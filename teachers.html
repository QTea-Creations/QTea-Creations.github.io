Curriculum Overview

Five missions. Five essential digital-safety skills.

Each mission includes a clear learning focus, interactive practice, and opportunities for classroom discussion.

1 Identity Protector Personal, private, and secret information +

Learning objective

Students will distinguish between information that may be shared safely and information that should remain private or secret.

Key vocabulary

  • Personal information
  • Private information
  • Secret information
  • Trusted adult
  • Digital identity

Discussion prompt

What clues could someone gather from a username, photograph, profile, or online post?

2 Password Safe Keeper Password strength and account protection +

Learning objective

Students will identify stronger password habits and explain how additional security steps help protect an account.

Key vocabulary

  • Password
  • Passphrase
  • Password reuse
  • Two-factor authentication
  • Account security

Discussion prompt

Why can a long passphrase be easier to remember and harder for someone else to guess?

3 Phish Finder Suspicious messages, links, and online offers +

Learning objective

Students will identify common warning signs in suspicious messages and explain why slowing down helps prevent mistakes.

Key vocabulary

  • Phishing
  • Scam
  • Suspicious link
  • Urgency
  • Verification

Discussion prompt

Why might a scammer create fear, excitement, or urgency before asking someone to click?

4 Digital Footprint Defender Posts, privacy, audiences, and online reputation +

Learning objective

Students will explain how online actions create a digital footprint and how posts may reach audiences beyond the original viewer.

Key vocabulary

  • Digital footprint
  • Online reputation
  • Audience
  • Screenshot
  • Permanence

Discussion prompt

How can a screenshot change who sees a post and how long it remains available?

5 Cyber Responder Saving evidence, blocking, reporting, and seeking help +

Learning objective

Students will select appropriate response steps when an online situation becomes unsafe, harmful, or confusing.

Key vocabulary

  • Evidence
  • Screenshot
  • Block
  • Report
  • Trusted adult

Discussion prompt

Why may it be important to save evidence before blocking or reporting an account?

Suggested Lesson Flow

A simple structure for using each mission.

Adjust the timing based on your students, class period, and instructional goals.

1

5–10 minutes

Introduce

Define the key concept and connect it to situations students may recognize.

2

10–20 minutes

Explore

Let students work through the related Safetii Net mission or activity.

3

5–10 minutes

Discuss

Ask students to explain what they noticed and why they made particular choices.

4

5 minutes

Reflect

End with an exit question, written response, or class safety commitment.

Flexible Teaching Formats

Adapt Safetii Net to your classroom.

🧑‍💻

Individual learning

Students independently complete a mission and record one important lesson or question.

  • Independent practice
  • Early-finisher activity
  • Technology station
👥

Small-group learning

Students discuss scenarios together and explain which digital-safety response they would choose.

  • Partner discussion
  • Learning stations
  • Intervention groups
🏫

Whole-class learning

Project a scenario, collect student responses, and lead a guided conversation about the safest choice.

  • Direct instruction
  • Advisory period
  • Digital-citizenship unit

Classroom Discussion

Ask students to explain their thinking.

Cyber-safety discussions become more meaningful when students move beyond naming the correct answer and explain why a choice is safer.

01

What clues helped you decide?

02

What could happen if someone chose differently?

03

Which trusted adult could help in this situation?

04

What would make this situation safer?

05

How might this choice affect another person?

Teaching Reminder

Avoid asking students to reveal real private information.

Use fictional examples when discussing passwords, addresses, usernames, accounts, or personal situations. Students should never be required to share actual passwords or sensitive information during a lesson.
